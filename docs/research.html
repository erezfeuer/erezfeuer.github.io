Research

I study how climate shapes the growth, function, and survival of trees — from the slow pressure of shifting averages to the sharp blows of drought and heat, and the long shadow these leave behind. My work combines ecological and climate time-series analysis, statistical and machine-learning models, and dynamical-systems thinking, drawing on dendrometer records, flux-tower data, global tree-ring archives, and high-resolution climate datasets.

Across projects, a single question recurs: how does a changing climate reshape the long-term trajectory of tree growth, function, and survival? I approach it from several directions at once — data-driven, mechanistic, and instrumental — because the answer lives at the intersection of all three.

Disentangling soil and atmospheric drought

Drought is not one thing. Trees experience it both through the soil (falling water availability at the roots) and through the atmosphere (rising vapor pressure deficit that pulls water from the canopy). These pressures usually arrive together, which makes their separate effects hard to disentangle — yet they act on the tree through different mechanisms.

In a semi-arid pine forest, I used high-resolution dendrometer measurements together with an irrigation experiment — where relieving soil drought isolates the effect of atmospheric drought — to separate how each shapes the daily cycles of stem growth, contraction, and recovery. A central result: heatwaves consistently depress stem expansion, but the consequence depends on soil water. Trees with a soil-water buffer merely slow down, while trees already at the edge are pushed into net contraction — losing water and shrinking rather than growing.

Losses outlast gains: a century of tree growth under climate change

Under climate change, will forests grow more or less? The answer differs from place to place, which has long obscured the overall direction. To trace it, we learned nonlinear climate–growth relationships from 4,110 Northern Hemisphere tree-ring chronologies recorded since 1902 — more than 12 million ring-width measurements — and projected them through the twenty-first century under CMIP6 climate scenarios.

Two findings stand out:

  • Across the Northern Hemisphere, projected growth declines, driven by warming and rising atmospheric water demand whose negative effect outweighs the gains from changing precipitation.
  • The response is asymmetric in time: once losses emerge they are rarely reversed, whereas gains tend to fade. The pattern holds in every well-sampled region and strengthens under stronger warming, with the sharpest declines in the Mediterranean and western and central Europe.

Where the tree-ring record can speak for a changing climate

The International Tree-Ring Data Bank is the largest archive of tree-ring records on Earth, and it underpins much of what we know about how forests respond to climate. But it was never designed as a climatic sample: some climates are densely represented, others barely at all. This project asks how well the record covers the climates trees actually experience, and how that coverage shifts as the climate warms.

Two ideas sit at its core. The first is space-for-time substitution — the common assumption that spatial climate gradients can stand in for change over time, that a warmer place tells us about a warmer future; I examine how far that holds across the network, where places are interchangeable and where they are not. The second is novel climates: as warming pushes forests toward conditions with no analog in the existing record, parts of the world move outside the sampled envelope. Mapping where coverage is dense versus sparse shows where the record can support confident inference, and where new sampling would most improve our ability to anticipate how forests respond.

Drought history reshapes tree survival under recurrent water stress

I am developing a minimal dynamical model of two coupled quantities that govern how a tree endures drought: its accessible carbon reserves and the functional capacity of its water-transport system. The two are linked. During drought a tree cannot fix carbon while it continues to respire, and its hydraulic system is damaged at the same time; restoring that capacity draws further on the same carbon reserves. A drought therefore leaves the tree in a different state — lower carbon, impaired hydraulics — and the next drought acts on that altered state rather than on a healthy one.

The model is a deliberate reduction of an established carbon–hydraulic framework, kept to the few processes that could let this history matter. Under ordinary conditions it has a healthy equilibrium and a threshold — a tipping point, or point of no return — that separates states that recover from states whose carbon is drawn down without recovering. This threshold is not imposed; it emerges from the carbon–hydraulic feedback itself. A drought reshapes the phase space for as long as it lasts, driving the tree toward that threshold; when it lifts, the flow relaxes to normal, but the tree resumes from wherever it was left.

Because a tree meets not a single drought but a regime of them, the question is how survival depends on the intensity, frequency, and duration of droughts and the timing between them. Events that are each survivable in isolation can carry a tree across the point of no return when they fall close together, before hydraulic capacity has been rebuilt — so that the sequence, and not the cumulative stress, sets the outcome. What I am after is whether carbon–hydraulic feedback alone is enough to make that history matter.

Future directions

My longer-term interest is the mechanistic basis of tree growth and mortality under climate change — how the twin currencies of tree health, carbon and water, are drawn down as the climate shifts, and how that determines which trees recover and which cross the line into decline.

I want to keep working across scales and methods: from individual-tree physiology measured at high resolution, to continental patterns in the tree-ring record, to mechanistic models that tie the two together. The unifying goal is to understand, and eventually forecast, forest resilience and vulnerability — not just how trees respond to the climate they have known, but how they will fare in the climates they are about to meet.
