Research

I study how trees respond to climate extremes — heatwaves, drought, and the shifting baselines of a warming world — and what those responses tell us about resilience, recovery, and mortality. My work combines ecological and climate time-series analysis, statistical and machine-learning models, and dynamical-systems thinking, drawing on dendrometer records, flux-tower data, global tree-ring archives, and high-resolution climate datasets.

Across projects, a single question recurs: how do short-term climate extremes reshape the long-term trajectory of tree growth, function, and survival? I approach it from several directions at once — data-driven, mechanistic, and instrumental — because the answer lives at the intersection of all three.

Disentangling soil and atmospheric drought

How trees grow, shrink, and recover under two different kinds of drought.

Drought is not one thing. Trees experience it both through the soil (falling water availability at the roots) and through the atmosphere (rising vapor pressure deficit that pulls water from the canopy). These two pressures often arrive together, which makes their individual effects hard to separate — yet they act on the tree through different mechanisms.

Using high-resolution dendrometer time series, I disentangle how soil versus atmospheric drought drive the daily cycles of stem growth, contraction, and recovery. The work shows how each type of drought leaves a distinct signature on stem dynamics, and how trees recover once conditions ease.

This is the first published chapter of my PhD.

👉 Feuer et al. (2025), Plant, Cell & Environment

Climate coverage of the global tree-ring network

Where the tree-ring record represents the world’s climates — and where climate change is moving beyond it.

The International Tree-Ring Data Bank (ITRDB) is the largest archive of tree-ring records on Earth, and it underpins much of what we know about how forests respond to climate. But the network was not designed as a climatic sample: some climates are densely represented, others barely at all.

This project asks how well the ITRDB covers the range of climates trees actually experience, and how that coverage shifts as the climate warms. Two ideas sit at its core:

  • Space-for-time substitution. Much of forest ecology assumes that spatial climate gradients can stand in for change over time — that a warmer place tells us about a warmer future. I test how far that substitution actually holds across the tree-ring network: where places are interchangeable, and where they are not.
  • Novel climates. As warming pushes trees into climatic conditions with no analog in the existing record, parts of the world move outside the sampled envelope. I map where coverage is dense versus sparse, identify the emerging novel climates the ITRDB cannot yet speak to, and highlight where new sampling would most improve our ability to anticipate forest responses.

The result is a map of both the reach and the blind spots of one of ecology’s most important datasets, at a moment when those blind spots are growing.

A dynamical-systems model of tree mortality

Treating survival and death as stable states — and asking what tips a tree from one to the other.

Building on and extending the framework of Trugman and colleagues, this project models tree survival as a dynamical system with more than one stable state. A tree carrying healthy carbon reserves and intact water-transport tissue sits in a “living” basin; a tree that is carbon-starved or hydraulically failed sits in a “mortality” basin. Between them lies a separatrix — a threshold that, once crossed, is hard to come back from.

The model tracks two coupled state variables through time:

  • Nonstructural carbohydrate (NSC) reserves — the tree’s stored energy budget.
  • Xylem / hydraulic integrity — the functioning of its water-transport system.

Disturbances — droughts and heatwaves — reshape this phase space. I characterize them by their frequency, duration, and intensity, and ask how each dimension bends the system’s stable states and pushes a tree toward, or across, the mortality threshold. Because disturbances arrive in sequence, the model also captures how repeated shocks accumulate: how a tree weakened by one drought meets the next.

The aim is a deliberately simple, interpretable model — one that makes the mechanisms of resilience and tipping legible, rather than burying them in complexity.

Future directions

My longer-term interest is the mechanistic basis of tree growth and mortality under climate change — how the twin currencies of tree health, carbon and water, are drawn down by climate extremes, and how that determines which trees recover and which cross the line into decline.

I want to keep working across scales and methods: from individual-tree physiology measured at high resolution, to continental patterns in the tree-ring record, to mechanistic models that tie the two together. The unifying goal is to forecast forest resilience and vulnerability in a warming world — to understand not just how trees respond to the climate they have known, but how they will fare in the climates they are about to meet.
