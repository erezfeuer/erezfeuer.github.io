Research

I study how climate shapes the growth, function, and survival of trees — from the slow pressure of shifting averages to the sharp blows of drought and heat, and the long shadow these leave behind. My work combines ecological and climate time-series analysis, statistical and machine-learning models, and dynamical-systems thinking, drawing on dendrometer records, flux-tower data, global tree-ring archives, and high-resolution climate datasets.

Across projects, a single question recurs: how does a changing climate reshape the long-term trajectory of tree growth, function, and survival? I approach it from several directions at once — data-driven, mechanistic, and instrumental — because the answer lives at the intersection of all three.

Which way is forest growth headed?

Under climate change, will forests grow more or less? The honest answer is that it depends on where you look — and those local differences have tended to cancel out into a shrug, hiding whether there is any overall direction at all.

I ask whether that direction can be recovered. The approach is to let the data define the climate–growth relationship rather than assume it: I learn the nonlinear response of tree growth to temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric water demand from thousands of tree-ring records across the Northern Hemisphere, then push those relationships forward under climate projections to see where each forest lands. Reading the results not as a single snapshot but as trajectories through time turns out to matter — it separates losses that stick from gains that quietly fade.

Can the tree-ring record speak for a changing climate?

Almost everything we know about tree growth at large scales rests on one archive: the global network of tree-ring records. But that archive was never assembled to be representative — it was built site by site, often chasing strong climate signals in cold or dry places. So a natural question is what climates it can actually speak for.

I am building a climate-space audit of the archive: placing forests and the sampled records in a shared, multi-season climate space and asking how much of the climate that forests live in today — and will live in tomorrow — sits close to something we have actually measured. Two distinctions drive it: being near a sampled climate is not the same as being well supported by many of them; and a sampling bias that looks like a weakness today may, by chance, point in the same direction the climate is moving — pre-positioning the record for some future forests while leaving others increasingly off the map. The aim is a clear picture of where tree-ring–based inference stands on solid ground, and where new sampling would help most.

When do survivable droughts become lethal in sequence?

Tree death from drought is usually weighed one event at a time — how hot, how dry, how long. But trees live through sequences of droughts, and a drought that is survivable on its own can leave lasting marks: depleted carbon reserves, hydraulic damage, a thinned canopy. The next drought then arrives at a tree that is no longer the same tree.

I am developing a deliberately minimal dynamical-systems model of a tree’s carbon and hydraulic state to explore this memory. The question is not “how severe was the drought” but when does history tip the balance — when do successive events buffer one another, when do they compound, and when does incomplete recovery carry a tree past the point of return.

The picture below is the intuition behind the model. Imagine a plane whose axes are the tree’s water status and its carbon reserves. A healthy, self-restoring state sits in one corner, separated by a threshold — a line of no return — from states that slide toward death. A drought bends the flow and pushes the tree off its healthy state; when it passes, recovery begins, but from wherever the tree was left. A second drought that full health would have shrugged off can, if it lands before recovery is complete, carry the tree across that line. History, not just total severity, decides the outcome.

Reading drought from the stem, hour by hour

Drought reaches a tree two ways at once — through the soil, as water runs short at the roots, and through the atmosphere, as dry air pulls water from the canopy. They usually arrive together, which makes their separate fingerprints hard to tell apart.

I use trees’ own micro-movements to pull them apart. High-resolution dendrometers record a stem swelling and shrinking through the day, and pairing them with an irrigation experiment — where relieving soil drought leaves only the atmospheric signal — lets me read growth, water status, and recovery straight from the stem. It shows, among other things, that whether a heatwave merely slows a tree or tips it into shrinking depends on the soil water it has to fall back on.

Future directions

My longer-term interest is the mechanistic basis of tree growth and mortality under climate change — how the twin currencies of tree health, carbon and water, are drawn down as the climate shifts, and how that decides which trees recover and which cross the line into decline.

I want to keep working across scales and methods: from individual-tree physiology measured at high resolution, to continental patterns in the tree-ring record, to mechanistic models that tie the two together — and, where it helps, building the sensors that gather the data in the first place. The unifying goal is to understand, and eventually forecast, forest resilience and vulnerability: not just how trees respond to the climate they have known, but how they will fare in the climates they are about to meet.
